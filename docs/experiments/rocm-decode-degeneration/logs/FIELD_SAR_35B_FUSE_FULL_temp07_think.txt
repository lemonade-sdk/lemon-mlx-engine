Maxwell's equations are a set of four fundamental partial differential equations that describe how **electric and magnetic fields interact with electric charges, currents, and each other**. Formulated by Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** in the 1860s, they unified the previously separate theories of electricity and magnetism into a single, coherent framework and laid the foundation for modern physics and electrical technology.

---

### 🔹 The Four Equations (Conceptually)

Maxwell's equations are usually presented in two equivalent forms: **integral** (over closed surfaces/loops) and **differential** (local point-by-point). Below is a physical description of each, followed by the modern differential form.

1. **Gauss's Law for Electricity**  
   *Electric charges produce electric fields.*  
   The total electric flux through a closed surface is proportional to the net electric charge enclosed.  
   ➤ **Differential form:** `∇ · E = ρ / ε₀`  
   *(Divergence of E equals charge density divided by vacuum permittivity.)*

2. **Gauss's Law for Magnetism**  
   *There are no magnetic monopoles.*  
   Magnetic field lines always form closed loops; they never begin or end on a point.  
   ➤ **Differential form:** `∇ · B = 0`  
   *(Divergence of B is always zero.)*

3. **Faraday's Law of Induction**  
   *A changing magnetic field induces an electric field.*  
   This is the operating principle behind electric generators, transformers, and induction cooktops.  
   ➤ **Differential form:** `∇ × E = −∂B/∂t`  
   *(Curl of E equals the negative rate of change of B.)*

4. **Ampère-Maxwell Law**  
   *Electric currents and changing electric fields produce magnetic fields.*  
   Maxwell added the "displacement current" term to Ampère's original law, making it consistent with charge conservation.  
   ➤ **Differential form:** `∇ × B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t`  
   *(Curl of B equals current density plus the rate of change of E.)*

**Symbols:**  
`E` = electric field, `B` = magnetic field, `ρ` = charge density, `J` = current density, `ε₀` = vacuum permittivity, `μ₀` = vacuum permeability.

*(Note: Maxwell originally wrote these in component form. The modern vector calculus notation was developed later by Oliver Heaviside and Josiah Willard Gibbs.)*

---

### 🔑 Profound Consequences

1. **Prediction of Electromagnetic Waves**  
   When Maxwell combined the equations in a vacuum (no charges or currents), they yielded a wave equation showing that disturbances in E and B fields propagate at speed:  
   `c = 1 / √(μ₀ε₀)`  
   This matched the measured speed of light, leading Maxwell to conclude: **light is an electromagnetic wave**.

2. **Unification of Physics**  
   Maxwell's work unified electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theory. It was the first major unification in physics since Newton.

3. **Foundation for Special Relativity**  
   Maxwell's equations are naturally consistent with the speed of light being constant in all inertial frames. This tension with Newtonian mechanics directly inspired Einstein's 1905 theory of special relativity.

4. **Technological Revolution**  
   Every wireless technology (radio, TV, cell phones, Wi-Fi, radar), power grid, electric motor, transformer, and modern optics relies on Maxwell's equations.

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Context

- **Classical Theory:** Maxwell's equations are a *classical* (non-quantum) theory. At atomic scales, they are superseded by **Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)**, which treats light as photons and includes quantum effects.
- **No Magnetic Monopoles:** The equation `∇ · B = 0` implies monopoles don't exist. Despite extensive searches, none have been observed. (Their existence would modify this equation.)
- **Macroscopic Accuracy:** For virtually all engineering and everyday physics, Maxwell's equations are exact and incredibly robust.

Fourier analysis is one of the most profound and widely applied mathematical frameworks in science and engineering. At its core, it answers a deceptively simple question: **Can any complex function or signal be broken down into simpler, periodic pieces?** The answer, strikingly, is yes.

---

### 🔍 Core Intuition
Imagine hearing a chord on a piano. Your ear automatically separates it into individual notes (frequencies). Fourier analysis does this mathematically for any function: it decomposes a complicated waveform (in the **time domain**) into a collection of pure sine and cosine waves (in the **frequency domain**). This reveals hidden patterns, periodicities, and energy distributions that are invisible in the original representation.

---

### 📜 Historical Origin
Proposed by **Joseph Fourier** in 1807 while studying heat conduction, the idea was initially controversial. Critics (including Lagrange and Poisson) argued that not all functions could be represented by trigonometric series. Fourier insisted they could, provided you allowed infinite sums. The debate drove the development of rigorous analysis, leading to modern concepts like:
- **Dirichlet conditions** (when a Fourier series converges)
- **L² space** (square-integrable functions, where Fourier analysis is mathematically solid)
- **Measure theory** (Lebesgue integration, needed for full generality)

Fourier’s insight survived criticism and became foundational to mathematical physics.

---

### 📐 Mathematical Framework

#### 1. **Fourier Series** (Periodic Functions)
Any periodic function \( f(t) \) with period \( T \) can be written as:
\[
f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( a_n \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n t}{T}\right) + b_n \sin\left(\frac{2\pi n t}{T}\right) \right)
\]
The coefficients \( a_n, b_n \) are found by projecting \( f(t) \) onto each sine/cosine basis function (orthogonality makes this tractable).

#### 2. **Fourier Transform (FT)** (Aperiodic/Continuous Signals)
For non-periodic signals, we let the period \( T \to \infty \), turning discrete frequencies into a continuous spectrum. The forward and inverse transforms are:
\[
\hat{f}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} \, dt \quad \text{(Frequency domain)}
\]
\[
f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{f}(\omega) e^{i\omega t} \, d\omega \quad \text{(Time domain)}
\]
*(Note: Scaling factors vary by field; some use \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \) for both to preserve symmetry.)*

#### 3. **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)** (Digital Signals)
For finite sequences \( x_0, \dots, x_{N-1} \):
\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \quad k=0,\dots,N-1
\]
This is the version computers actually compute.

---

### 🔑 Key Properties & Theorems
| Concept | Meaning | Why It Matters |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| **Convolution Theorem** | \( \mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g} \) | Turns painful integrals into simple multiplications. Crucial for filtering, optics, and solving linear PDEs. |
| **Parseval’s Theorem** | \( \int |f(t)|^2 dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int |\hat{f}(\omega)|^2 d\omega \) | Energy/power is conserved between domains. Foundation for signal analysis and compression. |
| **Duality & Symmetry** | Time ↔ Frequency, Scaling ↔ Stretching, Shift ↔ Phase | Reveals deep structural parallels; enables quick mental reasoning about signals. |
| **Uncertainty Principle** | \( \Delta t \cdot \Delta \omega \geq \frac{1}{2} \) | A signal cannot be sharply localized in both time and frequency. Sets fundamental limits on resolution. |

---

### 🌍 Real-World Applications
Fourier analysis is the **invisible engine** behind modern technology:
- **Signal Processing**: Audio compression (MP3), radio tuning, MRI (k-space reconstruction), radar/sonar
- **Image & Video**: JPEG compression (uses Discrete Cosine Transform, a Fourier variant), edge detection, filtering
- **Physics & Engineering**: Solving heat/wave equations, quantum mechanics (position ↔ momentum via FT), optics (diffraction patterns)
- **Data Science**: Time-series forecasting, anomaly detection, spectral analysis of financial markets
- **Cryptography & Coding Theory**: Fast polynomial multiplication, error-correcting codes (Reed-Solomon)

---

### ⚡ The Computational Revolution: FFT
The naive DFT scales as \( O(N^2) \), making it impractical for large datasets. In 1965, Cooley and Tukey (rediscovering Gauss’s 1805 work) introduced the **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)**, an algorithm that computes the DFT in \( O(N \log N) \). This turned Fourier analysis from a theoretical tool into a **real-time computational backbone** for everything from smartphones to radio telescopes.

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Extensions
Fourier analysis assumes signals are **stationary** (frequency content doesn’t change over time). This fails for:
- Speech, music, seismic signals, neural recordings
- Abrupt changes (Gibbs phenomenon: ringing near discontinuities)

**Modern alternatives/extensions:**
- **Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)**: Windows the signal to track frequency changes
- **Wavelet Transform**: Uses localized "wavelets" instead of infinite sines; excellent for multi-resolution analysis
- **Fractional & Fractional Fourier Transforms**: Generalize rotation in time-frequency space
- **Fourier Neural Operators**: Recent AI architecture for solving PDEs and learning operators in frequency domain

---

### 💡 Why It’s Profound
1. **Unification**: Turns differential equations into algebraic ones. Solves linear PDEs by diagonalizing operators.
2. **Duality**: Reveals that seemingly different domains (time/space vs. frequency/wavenumber) are mathematically equivalent.
3. **Universality**: Appears in quantum mechanics (position/momentum), optics (spatial frequencies), finance (periodic cycles), and even music theory (harmonic series).
4. **Computational Viability**: The FFT made it possible to analyze terabytes of data in real time.

The **Doppler effect** (or Doppler shift) is the change in frequency (or wavelength) of a wave perceived by an observer moving relative to the wave source. It applies to *all* wave phenomena: sound, light, water, seismic waves, and even quantum matter waves.

---

### 🔍 Core Intuition
When a source and observer move toward each other, wavefronts bunch up → higher frequency (shorter wavelength). When they separate, wavefronts stretch → lower frequency (longer wavelength).  
🎵 *Classic example:* An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches, then drops in pitch as it passes and recedes.

---

### 📐 The Mathematics

#### 1. **Classical Doppler (Waves in a Medium)**
Applies to sound, water waves, etc., where a medium defines a rest frame.  
Let:
- \(f_s\) = source frequency
- \(c\) = wave speed in medium
- \(v_o\) = observer speed (positive toward source)
- \(v_s\) = source speed (positive toward observer)

\[
f_{\text{obs}} = f_s \left( \frac{c + v_o}{c - v_s} \right)
\]
⚠️ Signs flip based on direction. This formula fails near light speed and doesn't apply to light (no medium).

#### 2. **Relativistic Doppler (Light/EM Waves)**
Maxwell's equations imply light speed \(c\) is invariant in all inertial frames. Special relativity modifies the shift:
- **Longitudinal** (motion along line of sight):
\[
f_{\text{obs}} = f_s \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \text{(approaching)}
\]
\[
f_{\text{obs}} = f_s \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} \quad \text{(receding)}
\]
where \(\beta = v/c\).  
- **Transverse Doppler**: Even with purely perpendicular motion, frequency shifts due to time dilation: \(f_{\text{obs}} = f_s / \gamma\). This is purely relativistic (no classical analog).

### 🔗 The Hidden Link to Fourier Analysis & Maxwell
This is where the physics becomes beautifully interconnected:

1. **Doppler = Frequency Translation in Fourier Space**  
   A Doppler shift doesn't change the *shape* of a signal's spectrum; it **shifts it**:  
   \[
   \mathcal{F}\{s(t)\} = S(f) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{F}\{s(t)e^{i 2\pi f_D t}\} = S(f - f_D)
   \]  
   This is why **FFT-based spectrum analysis** is the standard method for detecting Doppler shifts in radar, sonar, and communications. The shift appears as a peak displacement in the frequency domain.

2. **Maxwell's Equations → Relativistic Doppler**  
   EM waves satisfy the wave equation derived from Maxwell's equations. Because light speed is fixed, the classical Doppler formula violates causality at high speeds. Relativistic time dilation + Lorentz invariance of the wave phase \((\omega t - \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r})\) forces the square-root formula. The Doppler shift is essentially a **Lorentz boost in frequency space**.

3. **Wavefront Compression = Phase Modulation**  
   In signal processing terms, Doppler shift is a continuous phase modulation:  
   \[
   \phi(t) = 2\pi f_s t \quad \xrightarrow{\text{motion}} \quad \phi(t) = 2\pi f_s t + 2\pi f_D t
   \]  
   This connects directly to Fourier analysis via the **instantaneous frequency** concept: \(f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{d\phi}{dt}\).

### ⚠️ Nuances & Common Pitfalls
- **Cosmological redshift ≠ Doppler shift**: Galaxy redshifts come from metric expansion of space (FLRW metric), not kinematic motion through space. They're often confused but mathematically distinct.
- **Transverse motion**: Classical Doppler predicts zero shift for purely perpendicular motion. Relativity predicts a redshift (time dilation). Verified via Mössbauer rotor experiments and particle accelerators.
- **Doppler broadening**: Thermal motion in gases causes a distribution of shifts → spectral line broadening (used in plasma diagnostics and astrophysics).
- **Superluminal phase velocities**: The Doppler shift doesn't violate causality; phase velocity can exceed \(c\), but information travels at group velocity \(\leq c\).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.signal import lfilter

# ==========================================
# 1. MAXWELL'S EQUATIONS: WAVE GENERATION
# ==========================================
# EM waves in vacuum satisfy: ∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t²
# Analytical solution: E(t) = E₀ cos(2πf₀t) propagating at c
c = 3e8          # speed of light (m/s)
f0 = 10e9        # radar carrier frequency (Hz)
v_target = 50    # target radial velocity (m/s), + = approaching

# ==========================================
# 2. DOPPLER EFFECT: FREQUENCY SHIFT
# ==========================================
# Classical radar Doppler shift (round-trip path):
# f_D = 2 * v / c * f₀
f_D = 2 * v_target / c * f0
f_rx = f0 + f_D

# ==========================================
# 3. FOURIER ANALYSIS: SPECTRAL DECODING
# ==========================================
fs = 100e6       # ADC sampling rate (Hz)
T = 0.005        # observation window (s)
t = np.arange(0, T, 1/fs)

# Received EM wave (simplified: just frequency-shifted)
E_rx = np.cos(2 * np.pi * f_rx * t)

# Heterodyne mixing: multiply with reference to shift to baseband
# cos(2πf_rx t) * cos(2πf0 t) = 0.5[cos(2πf_D t) + cos(2π(2f0+f_D)t)]
E_baseband_raw = E_rx * np.cos(2 * np.pi * f0 * t)

# Low-pass filter to remove high-frequency sum term (~2f0)
# Using a simple first-order RC-like filter for demonstration
fc = 5000        # filter cutoff (Hz) >> f_D but << 2f0
b, a = lfilter.butter(2, fc/(fs/2), btype='low')
E_baseband = lfilter(b, a, E_baseband_raw)

# ==========================================
# 4. FOURIER TRANSFORM & VELOCITY EXTRACTION
# ==========================================
N = len(t)
freqs = np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=1/fs)
idx_pos = freqs > 0

# Power spectrum (squared magnitude)
power_spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.fft(E_baseband))**2

# Find peak → estimate Doppler frequency
idx_peak = np.argmax(power_spectrum[idx_pos])
f_D_est = freqs[idx_pos][idx_peak]
v_est = c * f_D_est / 2

# ==========================================
# PLOTTING: THE TRIAD IN ACTION
# ==========================================
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 8))

plt.subplot(2, 2, 1)
plt.plot(t[:500], E_rx[:500], label='Received EM wave', color='steelblue')
plt.xlabel('Time (s)'); plt.ylabel('Amplitude'); plt.title('1. EM Wave (Maxwell)')
plt.grid(); plt.legend()

plt.subplot(2, 2, 2)
plt.plot(t[:500], E_baseband[:500], label='Baseband after mixing', color='darkorange')
plt.xlabel('Time (s)'); plt.ylabel('Amplitude'); plt.title('2. Doppler Shift Applied')
plt.grid(); plt.legend()

plt.subplot(2, 2, 3)
plt.plot(freqs[idx_pos], power_spectrum[idx_pos], label='Power Spectrum', color='mediumseagreen')
plt.axvline(f_D_est, color='crimson', linestyle='--', label=f'Peak: {f_D_est:.2f} Hz')
plt.xlabel('Frequency (Hz)'); plt.ylabel('Power'); plt.title('3. Fourier Decoding')
plt.grid(); plt.legend()

plt.subplot(2, 2, 4)
plt.bar(['True v', 'Estimated v'], [v_target, v_est], color=['skyblue', 'orange'], edgecolor='black')
plt.ylabel('Velocity (m/s)'); plt.title(f'Velocity Extraction\nTrue: {v_target} m/s | Est: {v_est:.2f} m/s')
plt.grid(axis='y')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"True Doppler shift: {f_D:.2f} Hz")
print(f"Estimated Doppler shift: {f_D_est:.2f} Hz")
print(f"True target velocity: {v_target} m/s")
print(f"Estimated target velocity: {v_est:.2f} m/s")
