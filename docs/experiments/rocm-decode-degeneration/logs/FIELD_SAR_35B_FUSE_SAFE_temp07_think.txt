**Fourier analysis** is a mathematical framework that decomposes complex functions or signals into sums of simple, periodic components (sines and cosines). It reveals the **frequency content** of a signal and is one of the most powerful and widely used tools in science, engineering, and data analysis.

---

### 🔑 Core Idea
Any reasonably well-behaved function can be represented as a combination of oscillations at different frequencies. Instead of asking *"What is the value at time t?"* (time/space domain), Fourier analysis asks *"What frequencies are present, and how strong are they?"* (frequency domain).

---

### 📐 Mathematical Foundations

#### 1. **Fourier Series** (for periodic functions)
If \( f(x) \) is periodic with period \( L \), it can be written as:
\[
f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[ a_n \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n x}{L}\right) + b_n \sin\left(\frac{2\pi n x}{L}\right) \right]
\]
Coefficients are found via:
\[
a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n x}{L}\right) dx, \quad
b_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \sin\left(\frac{2\pi n x}{L}\right) dx
\]
*Used for: periodic waveforms, vibrations, AC circuits, thermal steady states.*

#### 2. **Fourier Transform (FT)** (for aperiodic/continuous functions)
\[
\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, e^{-2\pi i \xi x} \, dx
\]
**Inverse FT:**
\[
f(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{f}(\xi) \, e^{2\pi i \xi x} \, d\xi
\]
- \( \xi \) = frequency (Hz or rad/m)
- \( \hat{f}(\xi) \) = complex amplitude at frequency \( \xi \) (magnitude + phase)
*Used for: signal processing, optics, quantum mechanics, solving PDEs.*

> 📝 *Note: Conventions vary (factors of \( 2\pi \), sign in exponent, angular frequency \( \omega \)). All are mathematically equivalent.*

---

### 🧠 Key Properties & Theorems
| Concept | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Convolution Theorem** | \( \mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g} \) &nbsp; (convolution in time ↔ multiplication in frequency) |
| **Parseval's Theorem** | Energy is conserved: \( \int |f(x)|^2 dx = \int |\hat{f}(\xi)|^2 d\xi \) |
| **Linearity** | \( \mathcal{F}\{af + bg\} = a\hat{f} + b\hat{g} \) |
| **Frequency Shift** | \( \mathcal{F}\{f(x)e^{2\pi i \xi_0 x}\} = \hat{f}(\xi - \xi_0) \) |
| **Time-Frequency Uncertainty** | A signal cannot be arbitrarily localized in both time and frequency (basis of Heisenberg principle & wavelets) |

---

### 💻 Discrete & Computational Forms
Real-world data is sampled, so we use:
- **DFT (Discrete Fourier Transform)**: For \( N \) samples \( x[n] \)
  \[
  X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \, e^{-2\pi i kn/N}, \quad k=0,\dots,N-1
  \]
- **FFT (Fast Fourier Transform)**: An \( O(N \log N) \) algorithm to compute the DFT efficiently. Revolutionized digital signal processing.
- **STFT / Wavelets**: For non-stationary signals (frequency changes over time)

The **Doppler effect** is the change in observed frequency (or wavelength) of a wave when there is relative motion between the source of the wave and the observer. It applies to **all wave types**: sound, light, water, seismic, and radio waves.

---

### 🔍 Core Intuition
- **Approaching**: Waves get "compressed" → shorter wavelength → **higher frequency** (sound: higher pitch; light: "blueshift")
- **Receding**: Waves get "stretched" → longer wavelength → **lower frequency** (sound: lower pitch; light: "redshift")
- **No relative radial motion**: No shift (transverse motion only causes relativistic effects for light)

🎧 *Classic example*: An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches you, then drops in pitch as it passes and moves away.

---

### 📐 Mathematical Formulations

#### 1. **Classical Doppler (Sound/Waves in a Medium)**
General longitudinal formula:
\[
f' = f \left( \frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} \right)
\]
| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| \( f' \) | Observed frequency |
| \( f \) | Emitted frequency |
| \( v \) | Speed of wave in medium |
| \( v_o \) | Speed of observer (+ if moving toward source) |
| \( v_s \) | Speed of source (+ if moving toward observer) |

⚠️ **Important**: For sound, the medium matters. The observer's speed changes how fast they intercept wavefronts; the source's speed changes how closely spaced the wavefronts are.

#### 2. **Relativistic Doppler (Light/EM Waves in Vacuum)**
No medium is needed. Only **relative speed** \( v \) matters. For motion along the line of sight:
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \text{(approaching)}
\]
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} \quad \text{(receding)}
\]
where \( \beta = v/c \).

🔭 **Astronomy shorthand**: Redshift parameter
\[
z = \frac{\lambda' - \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c} \quad (v \ll c)
\]

# Simple moving average for zero-dependency demo
N_filt = 2000
s_bp = np.convolve(s_mix, np.ones(N_filt)/N_filt, mode='same')

# FFT → frequency domain decomposition

freq_axis = freqs[:len(freqs)//2]  # Positive frequencies only
mag = np.abs(S_mix[:len(S_mix)//2])

# Find spectral peak → estimate fD → compute velocity
idx_peak = np.argmax(mag)
fD_est = freq_axis[idx_peak]
v_est = fD_est * c / (2 * f0)

print(f"Estimated Doppler freq: {fD_est:.2f} Hz")
