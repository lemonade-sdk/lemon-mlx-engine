Maxwell's equations are a set of four fundamental partial differential equations that describe how **electric and magnetic fields** are generated, how they interact with matter, and how they propagate through space. Formulated by Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** in the 1860s (later condensed into their modern four-equation form by Oliver Heaviside and others in the 1880s), they unified electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single coherent theory and remain the cornerstone of classical electromagnetism.

---

### 🔹 The Four Equations (in vacuum)
Below is each equation with its physical meaning, mathematical form, and key implications. We use:
- `E` = electric field
- `B` = magnetic field
- `ρ` = electric charge density
- `J` = current density
- `ε₀` = vacuum permittivity
- `μ₀` = vacuum permeability
- `∇·` = divergence, `∇×` = curl, `∂/∂t` = partial time derivative

#### 1. **Gauss's Law for Electricity**
- **Meaning**: Electric charges produce electric fields. The total electric flux through a closed surface is proportional to the enclosed charge.
- **Equation**: `∇·E = ρ/ε₀`
- **Integral form**: `∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀`

#### 2. **Gauss's Law for Magnetism**
- **Meaning**: There are no magnetic monopoles. Magnetic field lines always form closed loops; they neither begin nor end.
- **Equation**: `∇·B = 0`
- **Integral form**: `∮ B·dA = 0`

#### 3. **Faraday's Law of Induction**
- **Meaning**: A changing magnetic field induces a circulating electric field. This is the principle behind generators and transformers.
- **Equation**: `∇×E = −∂B/∂t`
- **Integral form**: `∮ E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt`

#### 4. **Ampère–Maxwell Law**
- **Meaning**: Magnetic fields are produced by electric currents **and** by changing electric fields. Maxwell's addition (the "displacement current" term) was crucial.
- **Equation**: `∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t`
- **Integral form**: `∮ B·dl = μ₀I_enc + μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt`

---

### 🔑 Key Implications & Predictions
- **Electromagnetic waves**: When you combine these equations in free space (`ρ=0, J=0`), they predict self-sustaining waves of `E` and `B` fields propagating at speed:
  `c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s`
  This matched the known speed of light, leading Maxwell to conclude that **light is an electromagnetic wave**.
- **Unification**: Electricity, magnetism, and optics were shown to be different manifestations of a single phenomenon.
- **Relativistic consistency**: The equations are naturally compatible with Einstein's special relativity. In modern notation, they combine into two compact tensor equations: `∂_μ F^μν = μ₀ J^ν` and `∂_[α F_βγ] = 0`.
- **Foundation of technology**: Every wireless communication system, power grid, antenna, laser, and electronic circuit relies on these equations.

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Context
- **Classical theory**: Maxwell's equations describe fields continuously and deterministically. They break down at atomic scales where quantum effects dominate. There, they are superseded by **Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)**.
- **Macroscopic media**: In materials, the equations are often written using `D` (electric displacement) and `H` (magnetic field intensity) to account for polarization and magnetization: `∇·D = ρ_free`, `∇·B = 0`, `∇×E = −∂B/∂t`, `∇×H = J_free + ∂D/∂t`.
- **No quantum or relativistic corrections built-in**: They don't account for photon emission/absorption, vacuum polarization, or particle creation/annihilation.

---

Maxwell's equations are one of the great triumphs of 19th-century physics. They not only predicted radio waves (experimentally confirmed by Hertz in 1887) but also paved the way for special relativity and modern physics. Today, they remain indispensable in engineering, physics, and technology, accurate to extraordinary precision whenever quantum effects are negligible.

Fourier analysis is one of the most powerful and widely used mathematical frameworks in science and engineering. At its core, it provides a way to **decompose complex functions or signals into simpler oscillatory components** (sines and cosines), revealing their hidden frequency structure.

Think of it like a prism: just as a prism splits white light into its constituent colors, Fourier analysis splits a signal into its constituent frequencies.

---

### 🔑 Core Idea
Any "well-behaved" function can be expressed as a sum (or integral) of sinusoids. This is incredibly useful because:
- Sinusoids are **eigenfunctions of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems**: they pass through such systems unchanged except for scaling and phase shift.
- It turns **differential equations into algebraic equations**, making them far easier to solve.
- It reveals patterns invisible in the original domain (e.g., hidden periodicities, noise frequencies, resonances).

---

### 📐 Mathematical Foundations

#### 1. **Fourier Series** (Periodic Functions)
For a periodic function \( f(t) \) with period \( T \) (fundamental frequency \( \omega_0 = 2\pi/T \)):
\[
f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{i n \omega_0 t}
\]
where coefficients are found via orthogonality:
\[
c_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(t) e^{-i n \omega_0 t} \, dt
\]
*Real-valued signals* are often written as sums of sines and cosines (trigonometric form).

#### 2. **Fourier Transform (FT)** (Aperiodic/Finite-Energy Functions)
For non-periodic signals, the discrete sum becomes a continuous integral:
\[
\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} \, dt
\]
Inverse transform:
\[
f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega) e^{i\omega t} \, d\omega
\]
*(Note: Normalization conventions vary by field. Some use \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) for both, or \(f\) instead of \(\omega\).)*

---

### ⚙️ Key Properties
| Property | Time Domain | Frequency Domain |
|----------|-------------|------------------|
| **Linearity** | \(a f + b g\) | \(a F + b G\) |
| **Time shift** | \(f(t-t_0)\) | \(e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega)\) |
| **Frequency shift** | \(e^{i\omega_0 t}f(t)\) | \(F(\omega-\omega_0)\) |
| **Convolution** | \(f * g\) | \(F(\omega)G(\omega)\) |
| **Differentiation** | \(f'(t)\) | \(i\omega F(\omega)\) |
| **Parseval's** | \(\int |f|^2 dt\) | \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int |F|^2 d\omega\) (energy conserved) |

The **convolution theorem** is especially powerful: filtering in time = multiplication in frequency, and vice versa.

---

### 💻 Discrete & Computational Reality
Real-world signals are sampled → we use the **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)**:
\[
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-i 2\pi kn/N}, \quad k=0,\dots,N-1
\]
- The **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)** is not a new transform, but an \(O(N \log N)\) algorithm to compute the DFT efficiently.
- Requires careful handling of:
  - **Sampling rate & Nyquist limit** (avoid aliasing)
  - **Windowing** (reduce spectral leakage from finite data)
  - **Zero-padding** (interpolate frequency resolution)

---

### 🌍 Major Applications
- **Signal Processing**: Audio compression (MP3), image compression (JPEG), communications (OFDM, 5G), radar/sonar
- **Physics & Engineering**: Solving PDEs (heat, wave, Schrödinger equations), circuit analysis, vibration analysis, optics (diffraction, Fourier optics)
- **Data Science**: Time-series forecasting, feature extraction, anomaly detection, spectral analysis
- **Mathematics**: Harmonic analysis, number theory (Poisson summation), probability (characteristic functions)

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Extensions
- **Fixed resolution**: Standard FT assumes infinite duration. Real signals are transient → trade-off between time and frequency resolution.
- **Non-stationary signals**: Frequencies change over time → FT smears them.
- **Solutions**:
  - **Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)**: Windowed FT → spectrograms
  - **Wavelet Transform**: Multi-resolution analysis, better for transients/localized features
  - **Gabor Transform / Wigner-Ville**: Advanced time-frequency representations

---

### 🧠 Why It Matters
Fourier analysis is more than a tool; it's a **fundamental language of patterns**. It reveals that seemingly chaotic signals often hide simple periodic structures, and it bridges domains (time ↔ frequency, space ↔ spatial frequency, differential equations ↔ algebra). Its mathematical depth connects to group theory, functional analysis, and quantum mechanics, while its computational efficiency (FFT) powers virtually every digital system today.

The **Doppler effect** (or Doppler shift) is the change in frequency (or wavelength) of a wave perceived by an observer moving relative to the wave source. It’s the reason a siren’s pitch drops as an ambulance passes you, and why distant galaxies appear "redshifted."

---

### 🔹 Classical Doppler Effect (Mechanical Waves)
Applies to waves that require a medium (sound, water waves, seismic waves). The shift depends on the speeds of the source (\(v_s\)) and observer (\(v_o\)) relative to the medium, and the wave speed in that medium (\(c\)):

\[
f' = f \left( \frac{c \pm v_o}{c \mp v_s} \right)
\]

**Sign convention** (for motion along the line of sight):
- **Numerator**: \(+v_o\) if observer moves *toward* source, \(-v_o\) if *away*
- **Denominator**: \(-v_s\) if source moves *toward* observer, \(+v_s\) if *away*

*Example*: Ambulance approaching at 30 m/s, sound speed 340 m/s → higher pitch. Receding → lower pitch.

---

### 🔹 Relativistic Doppler Effect (Electromagnetic Waves)
Light and radio waves don’t need a medium, and at high speeds special relativity matters. For relative speed \(v\) along the line of sight (\(\beta = v/c\)):

**Approaching**:
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \lambda' = \lambda \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}
\]

**Receding**:
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \lambda' = \lambda \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}
\]
