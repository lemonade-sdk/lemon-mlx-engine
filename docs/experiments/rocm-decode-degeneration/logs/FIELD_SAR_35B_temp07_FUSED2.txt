Maxwell's equations are a set of four fundamental laws that describe how **electric and magnetic fields are generated, how they interact with each other, and how they respond to charges and currents**. Formulated by Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** in the 1860s, they unified electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theory of **classical electromagnetism**, and they remain one of the most beautiful and influential achievements in physics.

---

### 🔹 The Four Equations (Differential Form)
In modern notation (using vector calculus), they are usually written as:

| # | Name | Equation | Physical Meaning |
|---|------|----------|------------------|
| 1 | **Gauss's Law for Electricity** | `∇·E = ρ/ε₀` | Electric charges produce electric fields. The "divergence" of **E** (how much it spreads out) is proportional to charge density `ρ`. |
| 2 | **Gauss's Law for Magnetism** | `∇·B = 0` | There are no magnetic monopoles. Magnetic field lines always form closed loops; they never start or end. |
| 3 | **Faraday's Law of Induction** | `∇×E = −∂B/∂t` | A changing magnetic field creates a circulating electric field. This is the principle behind generators and transformers. |
| 4 | **Ampère–Maxwell Law** | `∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t` | Magnetic fields are produced by electric currents (`J`) **and** by changing electric fields (Maxwell's "displacement current" term). |

**Symbols:**
- `E` = electric field
- `B` = magnetic field
- `ρ` = electric charge density
- `J` = current density
- `ε₀` = vacuum permittivity
- `μ₀` = vacuum permeability
- `∇·` = divergence; `∇×` = curl; `∂/∂t` = partial time derivative

*(Note: These forms apply in a vacuum. In materials, `E` and `B` are replaced by `D` and `H` with material-dependent constants.)*

---

### 🔍 What These Equations Reveal

1. **Electromagnetic Waves**: When you combine Faraday's Law and the Ampère–Maxwell Law in empty space (`ρ=0, J=0`), they predict self-sustaining waves of **E** and **B** propagating together at speed:
   ```
   c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s
   ```
   This matched the known speed of light, leading Maxwell to famously conclude: **light is an electromagnetic wave**.

2. **Unification**: Electricity, magnetism, and optics were previously seen as separate phenomena. Maxwell showed they are different manifestations of a single electromagnetic field.

3. **Relativity & Symmetry**: The equations are naturally compatible with Einstein's special relativity. In fact, they were one of the first clues that the laws of physics should look the same to all observers moving at constant velocity.

---

### 📜 Historical Context
- Maxwell didn't invent the individual laws; he **synthesized** earlier work by Gauss, Faraday, Ampère, and others.
- His crucial insight was adding the **displacement current** term (`μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t`) to Ampère's law, ensuring charge conservation and wave solutions.
- The original 20-component formulation was later condensed into the compact 4-equation vector form we use today by **Oliver Heaviside** and **Hendrik Lorentz** in the 1880s–90s.

---

### 🌍 Modern Applications
Maxwell's equations underpin virtually all modern technology:
- 📡 Radio, TV, cell phones, Wi-Fi, radar
- 🔬 Particle accelerators, MRI machines, electron microscopes
- ⚡ Power generation & transmission, transformers, motors
- 🔭 Optics, lasers, fiber optics, photovoltaics
- 🧠 Theoretical physics: they motivated quantum electrodynamics (QED) and remain exact in the classical regime

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Beyond
- **Classical theory**: They don't account for quantum effects (e.g., photon quantization, spontaneous emission). That requires **Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)**.
- **Point charges & infinities**: Classical theory predicts infinite self-energy for point charges; renormalization in QED fixes this.
- **Magnetic monopoles**: Not predicted or observed; their existence would modify Gauss's law for magnetism to `∇·B = ρ_m`.

---

Maxwell's equations tell us:
- Charges create electric fields.
- Magnetic fields have no sources or sinks.
- Changing magnetic fields create electric fields.
- Currents and changing electric fields create magnetic fields.
- Together, they predict self-propagating electromagnetic waves moving at light speed, unifying optics with electricity and magnetism.

They are arguably the most important set of equations in classical physics, bridging 19th-century theory with 20th-century relativity and modern technology.

Fourier analysis is a foundational branch of mathematics that studies how **any function or signal can be decomposed into a sum of simple oscillating components** (sines and cosines, or complex exponentials). It transforms problems from the **time/space domain** into the **frequency domain**, where many operations become dramatically simpler.

---

### 🔑 Core Intuition
Think of a complex sound wave: it's not a single tone, but a mixture of many pitches played together. Fourier analysis asks: *"Which frequencies are present, and how strong is each one?"*  
It reveals that **smooth signals are dominated by low frequencies**, while **sharp transitions or noise require high frequencies**.

---

### 📐 The Mathematical Pillars

#### 1. **Fourier Series** (Periodic Functions)
For a periodic function \(f(t)\) with period \(T\):
\[
f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{i n \omega_0 t}, \quad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}
\]
Coefficients:
\[
c_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(t) e^{-i n \omega_0 t} \, dt
\]
*Convergence*: Works for piecewise smooth functions (Dirichlet conditions). Discontinuities cause Gibbs phenomenon (overshoot).

#### 2. **Fourier Transform (FT)** (Non-periodic / Infinite Domain)
\[
\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i 2\pi \xi t} \, dt \quad \text{(Forward)}
\]
\[
f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{f}(\xi) e^{i 2\pi \xi t} \, d\xi \quad \text{(Inverse)}
\]
\(\xi\) is frequency in Hz. (Note: conventions vary; some use \(\omega\), \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\), or \(e^{-i\omega t}\). All are mathematically equivalent.)

#### 3. **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)** (Digital Signals)
For \(N\) samples \(x_0,\dots,x_{N-1}\):
\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-i 2\pi kn/N}, \quad k=0,\dots,N-1
\]
Computed efficiently via the **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)**: \(O(N\log N)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).

---

### 🧠 Key Properties (Why It's Powerful)

| Property | Statement | Practical Use |
|----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Linearity** | \(\mathcal{F}\{af+bg\} = a\hat{f} + b\hat{g}\) | Superposition of signals |
| **Convolution Theorem** | \(\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g}\) | Turns differential/integral equations into algebra |
| **Shift** | \(\mathcal{F}\{f(t-t_0)\} = e^{-i2\pi\xi t_0}\hat{f}(\xi)\) | Time delay → phase shift in frequency |
| **Scaling** | \(\mathcal{F}\{f(at)\} = \frac{1}{|a|}\hat{f}(\xi/a)\) | Stretch in time → compress in frequency |
| **Parseval's Theorem** | \(\int |f(t)|^2 dt = \int |\hat{f}(\xi)|^2 d\xi\) | Energy conservation across domains |
| **Duality** | \(\mathcal{F}\{\hat{f}\}(t) = f(-\xi)\) | Symmetry between time & frequency |

---

### 🌍 Major Applications

| Field | How Fourier Analysis Is Used |
|-------|------------------------------|
| **Signal Processing** | Audio compression (MP3), image compression (JPEG), noise filtering, equalizers |
| **Communications** | Modulation/demodulation (AM/FM, OFDM), spectrum analysis, error correction |
| **Physics & Engineering** | Solving PDEs (heat, wave, Schrödinger), circuit analysis, optics (diffraction patterns) |
| **Data Science** | Time-series forecasting, feature extraction, spectral clustering, denoising |
| **Quantum Mechanics** | Position ↔ momentum duality via FT; wave-particle complementarity |

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Extensions

| Issue | Solution |
|-------|----------|
| **Non-stationary signals** (frequency changes over time) | Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), Wavelet Transform |
| **Spectral leakage** (finite window distorts frequencies) | Window functions (Hamming, Hanning), zero-padding |
| **Aliasing** (undersampling distorts high frequencies) | Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem: \(f_s > 2f_{\text{max}}\) |
| **Poor time-frequency resolution trade-off** | Gabor transform, chirp Z-transform, multiresolution analysis |

---

### 💡 Conceptual Takeaway
Fourier analysis is essentially a **change of basis** in function space: instead of representing a signal as values over time, you represent it as coefficients over frequencies. This exploits the fact that many physical systems are **linear and time-invariant**, making frequency domain analysis diagonalize their behavior.

The **Doppler effect** is the change in observed frequency (or wavelength) of a wave caused by relative motion between the source and the observer. It applies to all wave types: sound, light, water, seismic waves, etc.

---

### 🔹 Core Intuition
- **Source moving toward you** → waves get "bunched up" → **higher frequency** (blueshift / higher pitch)
- **Source moving away** → waves get "stretched out" → **lower frequency** (redshift / lower pitch)

**Familiar example**: An ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches, then drops in pitch as it passes and recedes.

---

### 📐 The Mathematics

#### 1. **Classical Doppler Effect** (Sound / Mechanical Waves)
Requires a physical medium. The formula depends on velocities relative to the medium:
\[
f' = f \,\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}
\]
- \(f\) = emitted frequency
- \(f'\) = observed frequency
- \(v\) = speed of wave in medium
- \(v_o\) = speed of observer (**+** if moving toward source)
- \(v_s\) = speed of source (**−** in denominator if moving toward observer)

*Rule of thumb*: Numerator ↑ when observer approaches; denominator ↓ when source approaches.

#### 2. **Relativistic Doppler Effect** (Light / EM Waves)
No medium needed. Time dilation modifies the shift. For motion along the line of sight:
\[
f' = f \,\sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \text{(approaching)}
\]
\[
f' = f \,\sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} \quad \text{(receding)}
\]
where \(\beta = v/c\) and \(c\) is the speed of light.

**Wavelength version** (often used in astronomy):
\[
\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} \approx \beta \quad \text{(for } \beta \ll 1\text{)}
\]
- \(\Delta \lambda > 0\) → **redshift** (receding)
- \(\Delta \lambda < 0\) → **blueshift** (approaching)

#### 3. **Transverse Doppler Effect**
Even if motion is perpendicular to the line of sight, relativity predicts a redshift purely from time dilation:
\[
f' = \frac{f}{\gamma} = f \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}
\]
This has no classical analog and was confirmed in particle accelerator experiments.

---

### 🌍 Major Applications

| Field | How Doppler Is Used |
|-------|---------------------|
| 🪐 **Astronomy** | Measure galaxy recession → Hubble's law & universe expansion; detect exoplanets via stellar wobble |
| 🚗 **Radar / Lidar** | Police speed guns, weather radar (precipitation velocity), air traffic control |
| 🏥 **Medical Imaging** | Doppler ultrasound: measures blood flow velocity & direction in arteries/heart |
| 📡 **Communications** | Satellite tracking, GPS clock corrections, FMCW radar in autonomous vehicles |
| 🔬 **Physics** | Laser cooling (Doppler cooling), plasma diagnostics, acoustic flow meters |

---

### 🔗 Connection to Fourier Analysis & Maxwell's Equations

- **Fourier link**: The Doppler effect is literally a **spectral translation**. If you take the Fourier transform of a Doppler-shifted signal, its spectrum shifts by \(\Delta f\). This is why spectrum analyzers and FFT-based radar can directly measure velocity.
- **EM link**: For light, the Doppler shift emerges naturally from **Lorentz-transforming the phase** \(\phi = \omega t - \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) in Maxwell's equations. The invariance of phase under Lorentz transformations guarantees the relativistic formula.
- **Practical FFT view**: A stationary source emits \(f_0\). A moving source produces \(f' \approx f_0 \pm \Delta f\). The beat frequency \(|f' - f_0|\) is directly proportional to velocity.

---

### ⚠️ Key Nuances & Limitations

| Issue | Explanation |
|-------|-------------|
| **Line-of-sight only** | Classical Doppler measures only radial velocity. Transverse motion requires relativistic treatment. |
| **Medium dependence** | Sound Doppler depends on wind/temperature; light Doppler does not. |
| **Sonic boom / Cherenkov** | When source speed ≥ wave speed, wavefronts pile up into a shock cone (Mach cone / Cherenkov radiation). |
| **Dispersion** | In dispersive media, phase velocity ≠ group velocity; Doppler formulas must specify which velocity applies. |

---

The Doppler effect quantifies how relative motion shifts wave frequency. For sound, it's classical and medium-dependent. For light, it's relativistic and vacuum-compatible. In practice, it's measured via spectral shifts (Fourier analysis) and powers everything from police radar to cosmology.

# Radar round-trip factor: fD = 2 * f0 * (v / c)
fD = 2 * f0 * (v / c)

# Received signal (delayed + shifted)
# For visualization, we model it as a pure frequency-shifted version:
rx = np.cos(2 * np.pi * (f0 + fD) * t)

# === 3. FOURIER ANALYSIS: SPECTRAL DECOMPOSITION ===
# Mix (multiply) tx and rx → trigonometric identity gives sum/difference frequencies
# cos(A)cos(B) = 0.5[cos(A-B) + cos(A+B)]
mixed = tx * rx

# Low-pass filter (remove ~2f0 terms, keep baseband)
# Simple moving average for demo (real systems use sharp digital filters)
N_lpf = int(fs / (f0 * 0.4))  # Cutoff ~ f0/2
h = np.ones(N_lpf) / N_lpf
baseband = np.convolve(mixed, h, mode='same')

# === 4. DOPPLER EXTRACTION via FFT ===
N = len(baseband)
freqs = np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=1/fs)
amp = np.abs(np.fft.fft(baseband))

# Find peak frequency (positive frequencies only)
idx = np.argmax(amp[N//2:]) + N//2
f_est = freqs[idx]

# Calculate estimated velocity
v_est = f_est * c / (2 * f0)

# === 5. VISUALIZATION ===
fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(10, 8))

# Time domain: Tx vs Received
axes[0].plot(t, tx, label='Transmitted (f₀)', alpha=0.7)
axes[0].plot(t, rx + 0.3, label=f'Received (f₀ + f_D)', alpha=0.7)
axes[0].set_ylabel('Amplitude')
axes[0].set_title('Time Domain: Transmitted vs Received Signal')
axes[0].legend()

# Mixed signal (before LPF)
axes[1].plot(t[:-N_lpf], mixed[:-N_lpf], color='purple', alpha=0.8)
axes[1].set_ylabel('Amplitude')
axes[1].set_title('Mixed Signal (Tx × Rx) - High-Frequency Content')

# Baseband after LPF + FFT spectrum
axes[2].plot(freqs[N//2:], amp[N//2:], color='red', linewidth=2)
axes[2].axvline(fD, color='green', linestyle='--', label=f'True f_D = {fD:.1f} Hz')
axes[2].axvline(f_est, color='blue', linestyle=':', label=f'Estimated f_D = {f_est:.1f} Hz')
axes[2].set_xlabel('Frequency (Hz)')
axes[2].set_ylabel('Magnitude')
axes[2].set_title('Doppler Spectrum (FFT of Baseband)')
axes[2].legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"✅ True Doppler frequency: {fD:.2f} Hz")
print(f"✅ Estimated Doppler frequency: {f_est:.2f} Hz")
print(f"✅ True velocity (normalized): {v:.3f}")
print(f"✅ Estimated velocity (normalized): {v_est:.3f}")

### 🔍 How This Maps to the Triad

| Step | Concept | What It Does |
|------|---------|--------------|
| **`tx` & `rx` generation** | **Maxwell** | Models EM wave propagation. The wave equation `∇²E - (1/c²)∂²E/∂t² = 0` has solutions like `cos(kx - ωt)`. Here we simulate 1D propagation with frequency `f₀`. |
| **`fD = 2f₀(v/c)`** | **Doppler** | Relates target velocity to frequency shift. The factor of 2 comes from round-trip propagation (radar). |
| **`mixed = tx * rx`** | **Fourier** | Multiplication in time = convolution in frequency. Splits signal into `f₀ ± (f₀+fD)` → low-frequency difference `fD` + high-frequency `2f₀+fD`. |
| **LPF + FFT** | **Fourier** | Removes high-frequency terms, isolates baseband. FFT diagonalizes the signal: peak at `fD` gives exact velocity. |

### 🧠 Key Engineering Insights

1. **Why downconvert?** Direct ADC at GHz is power-hungry. Real receivers mix down to baseband (kHz-MHz), then digitize. This code simulates that exact chain.
2. **FFT peak ≠ exact velocity** in practice: You’ll apply **windowing**, **zero-padding**, **interpolation** (e.g., Quinn’s method), or **FFT-based phase tracking** for sub-Hz precision.
3. **Range-Doppler coupling**: In FMCW radar, distance `R` and velocity `v` both shift frequency. The **2D FFT** (slow-time vs fast-time) produces a **Range-Doppler Map** (used in weather radar, autonomous driving).
