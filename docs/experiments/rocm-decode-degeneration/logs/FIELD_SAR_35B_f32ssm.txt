Formulated by Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** in the 1860s, they unified electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single coherent theory and laid the groundwork for modern physics and electrical engineering.

Below is the standard modern form. Each equation has a clear physical meaning:

| # | Name | Equation | Physical Meaning |
|---|------|----------|------------------|
| 1 | **Gauss's Law for Electricity** | `∇·E = ρ/ε₀` | Electric charges produce electric fields. The divergence of **E** at a point equals the charge density `ρ` there, scaled by the vacuum permittivity `ε₀`. |

| 3 | **Faraday's Law of Induction** | `∇×E = −∂B/∂t` | A changing magnetic field induces a circulating electric field. This is the principle behind generators and transformers. |
| 4 | **Ampère–Maxwell Law** | `∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t` | Magnetic fields are produced by electric currents (`J`) **and** by changing electric fields. Maxwell's addition (`μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t`) is called the *displacement current*. |

- `E` = electric field (V/m)
- `B` = magnetic field (T)
- `ρ` = electric charge density (C/m³)
- `J` = current density (A/m²)

- `μ₀` = vacuum permeability ≈ 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m

Fourier analysis is a mathematical framework that decomposes complex functions or signals into simpler oscillatory components (sines and cosines). Developed by **Joseph Fourier** in 1822 to solve the heat equation, it has become one of the most indispensable tools in science, engineering, and data science.

---

### 🔑 Core Idea
Any "well-behaved" function can be represented as a sum (or integral) of sinusoids with different frequencies, amplitudes, and phases. This shifts the problem from the **time/spatial domain** to the **frequency domain**, where operations like filtering, differentiation, or convolution often become algebraic instead of differential.

---

### 📐 Mathematical Framework

#### 1. Fourier Series (Periodic Functions)
For a periodic function \(f(t)\) with period \(T\):
\[
f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{i 2\pi n t / T}
\]
where the coefficients are:
\[
c_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T f(t) e^{-i 2\pi n t / T} \, dt
\]
*Convergence*: Holds under **Dirichlet conditions** (piecewise smooth, finite discontinuities). At jumps, the series converges to the average of left/right limits.

#### 2. Fourier Transform (Non-Periodic, Continuous Signals)
For functions in \(L^1(\mathbb{R})\) or \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\):
\[
\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i 2\pi \xi t} \, dt \quad \text{(Forward)}
\]
\[
f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{f}(\xi) e^{i 2\pi \xi t} \, d\xi \quad \text{(Inverse)}
\]
*Note*: Many fields use angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi\xi\), which removes the \(2\pi\) from the exponent but adds \(1/2\pi\) to the inverse. Both conventions are valid; just stay consistent.

#### 3. Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)
For \(N\) sampled points \(x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}\):
\[
X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \quad k = 0,\dots,N-1
\]
The DFT is the foundation of digital signal processing. The **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)** is an algorithm (not a new transform) that computes the DFT in \(O(N \log N)\) time instead of \(O(N^2)\), enabling real-time audio, image, and communications processing.

---

### ⚡ Key Properties & Theorems
| Property | Statement | Why It Matters |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| **Linearity** | \(\mathcal{F}\{af + bg\} = a\hat{f} + b\hat{g}\) | Superposition holds in frequency domain |
| **Convolution Theorem** | \(\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g}\) | Turns convolutions into multiplications; basis for filters & PDEs |
| **Differentiation** | \(\mathcal{F}\{f^{(n)}\} = (i 2\pi \xi)^n \hat{f}(\xi)\) | ODEs/PDEs become algebraic equations |
| **Parseval/Plancherel** | \(\int |f(t)|^2 dt = \int |\hat{f}(\xi)|^2 d\xi\) | Energy/power is conserved across domains |
| **Uncertainty Principle** | \(\Delta t \cdot \Delta \xi \geq \frac{1}{4\pi}\) | A signal can't be sharply localized in both time and frequency |

---

### 🌍 Real-World Applications
- **Signal Processing**: Audio compression (MP3), radio modulation, noise filtering
- **Image Processing**: JPEG (uses DCT, a Fourier variant), edge detection, MRI reconstruction
- **Physics & Engineering**: Solving heat/wave equations, quantum mechanics (position ↔ momentum), crystallography (X-ray diffraction)
- **Data Science**: Spectral analysis, time-series forecasting, anomaly detection
- **Cryptography & Number Theory**: Discrete Fourier variants over finite fields

---

### ⚠️ Limitations & Modern Extensions
The classical FT assumes infinite, stationary signals. Real-world data is often:
- **Non-stationary** (frequency changes over time) → Use **Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)** or **Wavelet Transform**
- **Discrete & finite** → Use **DFT/FFT** with windowing to avoid spectral leakage
- **Multi-scale** → Wavelets adapt resolution (high freq = short window, low freq = long window)

The **Doppler effect** (or Doppler shift) is the change in observed frequency (or wavelength) of a wave caused by relative motion between the source and the observer. It applies to all wave phenomena: sound, light, radio, water waves, and even quantum matter waves.

---

### 🔹 Core Concept
When source and observer move **toward** each other, wavefronts compress → higher frequency (blueshift for light, higher pitch for sound).  
When they move **apart**, wavefronts stretch → lower frequency (redshift, lower pitch).

---

### 📐 Mathematical Formulations

#### 1. Classical Doppler (Mechanical Waves, e.g., Sound)
For motion along the line of sight:
\[
f' = f \left( \frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} \right)
\]
- \(f'\) = observed frequency  
- \(f\) = emitted frequency  
- \(v\) = wave speed in medium  
- \(v_o\) = observer speed (＋ if moving toward source)  
- \(v_s\) = source speed (－ if moving toward observer)  

*Example*: Ambulance siren (\(f=1000\,\text{Hz}\)), \(v=340\,\text{m/s}\), source approaches at \(30\,\text{m/s}\):  
\(f' = 1000 \cdot \frac{340}{340-30} \approx 1097\,\text{Hz}\).

#### 2. Relativistic Doppler (Electromagnetic Waves)
Since light requires no medium and \(c\) is invariant, special relativity modifies the shift. For longitudinal motion:
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \text{(approaching)}
\]
\[
f' = f \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} \quad \text{(receding)}
\]
where \(\beta = v/c\). For \(v \ll c\), this approximates to \(f' \approx f(1 \pm v/c)\).

**Transverse Doppler** (motion perpendicular to line of sight):  
Pure time-dilation effect: \(f' = f / \gamma\), where \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\). First confirmed in particle accelerator experiments.
